ourSummary: >-
  In most US states, people who provide services to children or vulnerable adults are
  mandated reporters, and both codes require you to comply with the legal obligations that
  bind you. Three features of the duty are regularly misunderstood. The standard is
  reasonable suspicion, not proof — investigating is the agency's job, not yours. The duty
  is personal: telling a supervisor or an employer does not by itself discharge it, and an
  employer cannot forbid you from reporting. And good-faith reports are protected, which
  is the law's way of saying it would rather receive a report that turns out to be
  unfounded than miss one. The specific deadlines, hotlines and categories are set by your
  state's statute, so find them before you need them.
plainSummary: >-
  If you reasonably suspect abuse or neglect, report it to the state agency yourself. You
  do not need proof. Telling your boss is not enough on its own.
whatThisLooksLike:
  - Knowing your state's hotline number and your employer's procedure in advance.
  - Making the report the same day, then informing your supervisor.
  - Writing down what you saw or heard factually, in the person's own words where there were words.
  - Recording the time of the report and any reference number you were given.
commonPitfalls:
  - Waiting for certainty, which is not the legal standard.
  - Passing it to a supervisor and considering the duty done.
  - Investigating yourself, or asking the suspected person about it.
  - Promising a learner you will keep it secret.
ifYouAreUnsure: >-

  it does, and your state's agency can tell you in the same call.

Confidentiality does not override this. Mandated reporting is one of the situations where
the law requires disclosure, and both codes require you to follow the law.
