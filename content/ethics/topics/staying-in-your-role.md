ourSummary: >-
  A technician delivers behavior-analytic services designed by a supervisor who remains
  responsible for that work. That single fact decides most day-to-day ethics questions.
  Running the plan as written, collecting the data it specifies, and reporting what you
  observe are inside the role. Writing or modifying a plan, deciding to drop a procedure,
  interpreting assessment results for a family, or diagnosing anything are not — however
  sensible the change would be and however experienced you are. Suggestions are welcome
  and valuable; what makes them safe is that they go to the supervisor rather than into
  the session.
plainSummary: >-
  Run the plan as written. Record what happens. Report what you see. Changing the plan,
  interpreting results, or advising a family is your supervisor's job, not yours.
whatThisLooksLike:
  - Continuing the written procedure while raising your idea for improving it with your supervisor.
  - Telling a caregiver that the analyst decides program changes, and passing the request on that day.
  - Describing what happened in a session without explaining what it means.
  - Declining to answer a medication question and routing it to the medical team through your supervisor.
commonPitfalls:
  - Making a small change "just this once" because it obviously helps.
  - Answering "why does he do that?" with your own interpretation because a parent is upset.
  - Quietly dropping a step you disagree with, which makes the data uninterpretable.
  - Assuming years of experience change the role; the role is defined by the credential, not by tenure.
ifYouAreUnsure: >-

The frustrating version of this rule is when you can see exactly what would work and you
still have to wait. The reason it holds anyway is that consistency across everyone
working with a learner is most of what makes a plan effective, and the person who can see
all of that is the supervisor.
