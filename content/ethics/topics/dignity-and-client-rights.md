ourSummary: >-
  Dignity is one of the core principles, and it lives in ordinary details: speaking to
  someone rather than about them in their presence, protecting their privacy in front of
  other families, offering choices where the plan allows, and not running a person's day
  purely for the convenience of the setting. Assent is the specific version of this that
  comes up every session. A guardian gives consent; the learner gives or withholds assent,
  in whatever way they can — words, pushing materials away, turning their body, leaving.
  Assent withdrawal is information, not non-compliance, and a plan should say how to
  respond to it. Autistic adults have been consistent and public that compliance training
  which overrides those signals does lasting harm, and that critique has changed what
  good practice looks like.
plainSummary: >-
  Treat the learner as a person. Give choices where you can. Watch for signs that they
  have had enough, and treat those signs as information rather than as defiance.
whatThisLooksLike:
  - Offering a choice of two activities where the program allows it.
  - Pausing when a learner signals distress, and recording it for the supervisor.
  - Talking to a learner about what is happening next, whether or not they speak.
  - Not discussing a learner's toileting or behavior where others can hear.
commonPitfalls:
  - Reading every refusal as a behavior to be worked through.
  - Assuming consent from a guardian settles whether to continue in the moment.
  - Prioritizing trial counts over the learner's evident state.
  - Talking about someone in the third person while they are sitting there.
ifYouAreUnsure: >-
  Treat a signal of withdrawal as real, respond the way the plan directs, and report the
  pattern. If the plan has nothing to say about assent, that is itself worth raising with
  your supervisor.
