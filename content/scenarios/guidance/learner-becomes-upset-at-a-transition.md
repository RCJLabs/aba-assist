---
id: learner-becomes-upset-at-a-transition
kind: guidance
title: The learner becomes upset when it is time to move to the next activity
situation: >-
  A preferred activity is ending and a less preferred one is next. The learner cries,
  protests or refuses to move. Nobody is at risk and nothing is being damaged. The
  learner's plan includes transition strategies.
setting: school
audience: [RBT, BCaBA, BCBA]
riskFlags: []
steps:
  - text: Use the transition supports the plan specifies — a timer, a visual schedule, a warning — every time, not only when trouble is expected.
    rationale: >-
      Antecedent supports work by being predictable. Used inconsistently, they become
      a signal that something unpleasant is about to happen.
  - text: Keep your instruction short, deliver it once, and follow the plan's procedure for what happens next.
    rationale: >-
      Extra talking at a transition tends to become the attention or the delay that
      maintains the protest.
  - text: Follow the plan for reinforcing the transition when it happens, even if it was slow.
    rationale: >-
      The transition is the target behavior. What happens after it is what shapes the
      next one.
  - text: Record the transition — how long it took, what supports were used, and what happened.
    rationale: >-
      Latency to transition is exactly the kind of measure a supervisor uses to judge
      whether the supports are working.
whatNotToDo:
  - >-
    Do not extend the preferred activity to stop the protest unless the plan allows it;
    that teaches protesting as a way to keep the activity.
  - >-
    Do not skip the next activity because the transition was hard.
  - >-
    Do not add new supports of your own; suggest them to the supervisor instead.
whenToEscalate:
  - >-
    Tell your supervisor if transitions are getting longer or harder over several
    sessions, or if the plan's supports are not being used consistently by others.
