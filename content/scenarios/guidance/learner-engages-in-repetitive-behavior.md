title: The learner rocks, flaps, or repeats sounds during the session
situation: >-
  The learner engages in a repetitive movement or sound — rocking, hand flapping,
  humming, spinning an object. It is not hurting anyone and is not damaging anything. You
  are not sure whether you should do anything about it.
setting: clinic
audience: [RBT, BCaBA, BCBA]
riskFlags: []
steps:
  - text: Check whether the plan says anything about this behavior. If it does not, it is not a target, and you do not need to interrupt it.
    rationale: >-
      Repetitive behavior is often self-regulating and harmless. Interrupting behavior
      that is not in the plan is a program change, and it can be disrespectful.
  - text: Continue teaching. If the behavior interferes with a specific task, use the attending or redirection procedure the plan gives for that task.
    rationale: >-
      Many programs distinguish between behavior that competes with learning and
      behavior that merely accompanies it.
  - text: If the plan asks you to record the behavior, record it with the measure the plan specifies.
    rationale: >-
      Automatically reinforced behavior is hard to assess; consistent data are what make
      assessment possible.
  - text: Note anything you observe about when it increases — before demands, when alone, when tired.
    rationale: >-
      Those observations help the supervisor decide whether the behavior has a social
      function after all.
whatNotToDo:
  - >-
    Do not tell the learner to stop, or take the object away, unless the plan
    specifically says so.
  - >-
    Do not put your hands on the learner to interrupt it. Physical procedures need both
    the plan's authorization and the certified training your employer requires, and that
    training is not something a plan, a colleague or an app can supply.
  - >-
    Do not assume the behavior is a problem because it looks unusual.
  - >-
    Do not treat it as attention-seeking without evidence; automatically reinforced
    behavior does not need an audience.
whenToEscalate:
  - >-
    Tell your supervisor if the behavior is new, is increasing, is interfering with
    learning, or is starting to cause any physical wear.

Autistic adults have said clearly and repeatedly that repetitive movement is often
soothing, regulating, or simply pleasant. A plan that targets it should have a reason that
serves the learner, and a technician who is not sure whether it does should ask.
