id: celeration
term: Celeration
aliases: [acceleration, deceleration]
category: measurement
definition:
  technical: >-
    How fast a rate is changing, expressed as a factor per unit of time — a count per
    minute per week, say. Where rate answers how often the behavior happens, celeration
    answers whether that is rising or falling and how quickly, so it is a measure of
    learning rather than of performance on any one day. It is conventionally plotted on
    the Standard Celeration Chart, whose multiply-divide vertical scale turns a constant
    celeration into a straight line.
  plain: >-
    Not how much someone is doing, but how fast they are getting better or worse at it.
    Twenty a minute this week and thirty next week is a rising celeration.
  gloss: The rate at which a rate is changing
examples:
  - text: >-
      A learner reads 40 words a minute in week one and 60 in week two. Multiplying by
      1.5 each week is a celeration of ×1.5 per week.
    setting: school
    why: The figure describes the change between weeks, not the performance in either.
  - text: >-
      Screaming falls from 8 an hour to 4 an hour over two weeks. Dividing by 2 each
      week is a decelerating trend, and the number says how fast.
    setting: clinic
    why: A falling celeration is progress on a behavior targeted for reduction.
nonExamples:
  - text: >-
      "She does 60 words a minute." That is a rate — one session's performance — and says
      nothing about whether it is improving.
    setting: school
  - text: >-
      "The line on the graph goes up." That is a trend described in words. Celeration puts
      a number on how steep it is, which is what makes two programs comparable.
    setting: clinic

Celeration is the reason precision teaching charts rate on a multiply-divide scale rather
than an ordinary one: on that scale a constant celeration is a straight line, so learning
that is steady looks steady, and learning that has stalled is visible as a line going
flat rather than as a judgment call about a cloud of dots.

It is worth knowing even if you never draw one. A program can look fine on today's rate
and be going nowhere, and it can look poor today while improving quickly. Those are
different problems with different answers, and only the second measure tells them apart.
